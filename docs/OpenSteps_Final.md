# OpenSteps — Final Thesis & Phase 0 Brief

**OpenSteps creates independently verifiable receipts for AI agent actions, so auditors, customers, and counterparties can prove what happened without trusting the operator's logs.**

*Version 2.0 — Final pre-MVP document. June 2026. This supersedes all previous drafts. The next artifact after this one is working code.*

---

## 1. The thesis in one paragraph

AI agents now execute real actions — payments, journal entries, data exports, code changes — and the entire first wave of agent-security products (gateways, firewalls, identity brokers) shares one structural flaw: the audit trail is owned by the party being audited. "Tamper-evident" in today's market means a SOC 2 letter, not mathematics. OpenSteps is the verifiability tier that sits behind or beside any gateway: every agent tool call produces a signed, hash-chained Agent Action Receipt, and an open-source `verify` command lets any third party — an auditor, a client, a regulator, opposing counsel — check the record with no database access and no trust in the operator or in OpenSteps itself. We are not another gateway. We are the neutral evidence layer the gateways don't provide and are structurally reluctant to build.

## 2. What changed from v1.0 (feedback incorporated)

Four corrections from external review are now baked in. First, the claim "court-grade proof" is retired: courts do not automatically admit evidence because it is signed. The honest claim is **audit-grade, independently verifiable receipts, designed to simplify evidentiary authentication** — and that design goal has real legal substance, since US Federal Rules of Evidence 902(13) and 902(14) allow records generated by an electronic process, and data verified by hash, to be self-authenticating with a written certification rather than live testimony. We build toward that bar without overclaiming it. Second, the TEE is demoted from centerpiece to optional top rung of a trust ladder (Section 4); the MVP and the company's identity do not depend on it. Third, demand validation is now explicitly gated ahead of architecture: the first ten interviews matter more than any component described here, and the interview script is included in this document (Section 7). Fourth, the beachhead is narrowed from "regulated industries" to **the accounting and audit domain**, entered through agent vendors rather than the firms themselves (Section 6).

## 3. Problem

Enterprises deploying agents face exploding scrutiny: agentic AI is widely cited as the top attack vector of 2026, MCP server compromises produced thirty-plus CVEs in two months, and analyst estimates put millions of agents in production with thousands of agent identities per team. The market answered with 45+ gateway and guardrail vendors plus identity incumbents (Okta, Microsoft Entra, Aembit). All of them log; none of them produce evidence a skeptical outsider can verify. The gap becomes acute in three recurring situations: regulated retention regimes (HIPAA's ~six-year documentation requirements, seven-year financial-records norms) that demand logs protected from the very teams that generate them; cross-organization agent workflows, where no single party can own the log; and post-incident disputes, where the difference between "our database says" and "here is a receipt you can verify yourself" is the difference between an argument and evidence.

## 4. Solution: the trust ladder

OpenSteps offers one product with three escalating roots of trust, each adopted only when a customer's threat model demands it. This ladder is the company's core architectural idea and the answer to both "TEEs are too complex" and "software signatures are too weak."

**Rung 1 — Signed hash chain (the MVP).** Every tool call that passes the policy check becomes a receipt: agent identity, tool, canonicalized input hash, output hash, policy decision, human approver if any, timestamp, and the hash of the previous receipt, all signed with Ed25519. The chain makes deletion and reordering detectable; the signature makes forgery detectable. Storage is the customer's own bucket — the store needs no trust because integrity lives in the cryptography.

**Rung 2 — Transparency-log anchoring.** Chain heads are periodically published to a public append-only transparency log (Sigstore Rekor or equivalent). This gives third parties proof of inclusion and ordering — the operator cannot quietly drop receipts or rewrite history after the fact — at near-zero cost and with no enclave complexity. This rung alone defeats most realistic insider-tampering scenarios and is the default recommendation for compliance buyers.

**Rung 3 — TEE-attested signer.** For the highest-assurance tier (cross-org workflows, adversarial disputes), the signer and credential vault move into a TEE (AWS Nitro Enclaves first). Remote attestation binds the signing key to a measurement of the published, reproducibly built, open-source gateway code, so a receipt proves not just what was logged but that unmodified enforcement code produced it. Only the checkpoint runs in the enclave — never the model — so the system stays framework-agnostic and runs on commodity CPU confidential computing. Build trigger: at least three interviewees or design partners independently state that ordinary signed logs are insufficient for their evidence needs. Until then, Rung 3 is a roadmap slide, not code.

Honest framing at every rung: each step raises the attacker's cost — from "edit a database" to "compromise a public log" to "break CPU silicon" — and no single root of trust is load-bearing.

## 5. Phase 0 MVP specification

Scope: an open-source MCP proxy with policy checks, signed hash-chained receipts, and a standalone verifier. Two to four weeks of work. No TEE, no hosted service, no dashboard.

**Components.** A proxy that speaks MCP on both sides and wraps any existing MCP server; a policy file (YAML — allow/deny per tool, parameter constraints, and an `approve` action that pauses for human confirmation via CLI prompt in v0); a signer (Ed25519, key in a local file for v0, KMS later); an append-only receipt log (JSONL in a local directory or S3); and `opensteps verify`, a single binary that takes a receipt file or chain and a public key and returns a verdict, with no network and no account.

**Receipt schema (v0):**

```json
{
  "version": "0.1",
  "receipt_id": "uuid",
  "prev_hash": "sha256 of previous receipt, or null for genesis",
  "timestamp": "RFC 3339 UTC",
  "agent": { "id": "string", "framework": "string" },
  "tool": { "server": "string", "name": "string" },
  "request_hash": "sha256 of canonicalized (JCS) request params",
  "response_hash": "sha256 of canonicalized response, or null if blocked",
  "policy": { "rule_id": "string", "decision": "allow | deny | approve" },
  "approver": { "id": "string or null", "method": "cli | slack | none" },
  "chain_head_anchor": "transparency-log entry id, or null (rung 2)",
  "signature": "ed25519 over the canonicalized receipt body"
}
```

Canonicalization uses RFC 8785 (JCS) so independent implementations hash identically — this matters because the verifier must be rewritable by strangers, which is the whole point.

**The launch demo.** A scripted scenario: an agent doing bookkeeping work is prompt-injected via a malicious invoice into attempting an unauthorized payment; OpenSteps blocks it under a policy rule; the resulting receipt chain — including the blocked attempt — is exported and verified live by a third party running `verify` on their own machine. Written up against the OWASP Agentic Top 10 (the attempted action maps to ASI01 goal hijack and ASI02 tool misuse), this is the launch blog post, the conference demo, and the first thirty seconds of every customer conversation.

**Definition of done for Phase 0:** the demo runs end-to-end; the repo is public with the schema documented; a stranger has successfully run `verify` without help.

## 6. Beachhead: the accounting and audit domain, entered through vendors

The domain is right because the evidence need is native: agents are already posting journal entries, reconciling accounts, and pulling audit evidence on client financial data; the workflows cross a trust boundary (firm → client → regulator); retention and tamper-evidence requirements are explicit; and "receipts for what the AI did to the books" is explainable in one sentence to a non-technical partner.

The entry point, however, is not the audit firms themselves — large-firm procurement is slow and risk-averse, which is lethal on a student-founder timeline. The faster door into the same room is the **AI agent vendors selling into accounting**: the current wave of AI bookkeeping, audit-automation, and close-management startups. They must prove to their customers and their customers' regulators that their agents are trustworthy; they buy fast; receipts become their sales weapon rather than a compliance checkbox; and one vendor integration distributes OpenSteps across that vendor's entire client base. The interview pool therefore spans both groups — vendors and firms — and the interviews, not this document, choose the final entry point.

## 7. Validation plan and interview script

Ten interviews in the first four weeks, drawn from agentic-accounting vendors, audit/compliance staff at accounting firms, fintech compliance, and agent-platform security engineers. The discipline is Mom-Test style: ask about the past and the present, never about hypothetical willingness to pay, because everyone says yes to hypotheticals.

Core script, adapted per audience: "Walk me through the last time an auditor, client, or regulator challenged your logs or your automation's output — what did you have to produce, how long did it take, what did it cost?" "How do you currently prove what your AI/automation did versus what a human did?" "Who, specifically, asks for that proof — and what happens if you can't provide it?" "Have you ever suspected a log was wrong, incomplete, or edited? What did you do?" "What do you use today for this — gateway logs, SIEM, screenshots, attestations — and what's the worst part of it?" "If a tool gave your *clients* the ability to verify your agent's actions themselves, would that help you sell, or scare them?" (vendor-specific) and "Who owns the budget when an audit-evidence gap is discovered?"

**Decision gates, stated in advance.** Proceed toward a company if at least three interviewees describe a concrete past incident where log trustworthiness cost them time, money, or a deal, and at least one asks to use the MVP. Build Rung 3 (TEE) only if at least three independently say signed logs are not strong enough proof. Pivot to the open-source/standards path — or wind down to a portfolio project — if the consistent signal is "nice to have" with no incident stories and no budget owner. These gates are pre-committed precisely so enthusiasm in the room can't move them later.

## 8. Competition, condensed

Gateways and firewalls (MintMCP, Bifrost, TrueFoundry, Arcade, MCP Manager and ~40 more) enforce and log but anchor trust in their own stores and certifications — they are channel partners for the signer, not enemies. Identity and NHI platforms (Okta, Entra Agent ID, Aembit, YC-stage Alter/Multifactor) own who the agent is; their audit trails live in their clouds and are not third-party verifiable. Confidential-AI platforms (NEAR AI, Opaque, Tinfoil, Phala) attest the model — heavy GPU enclaves for a different problem; OpenSteps attests the checkpoint and stays framework-agnostic. Open-source primitives and research (AgentSeal, ArkForge receipts, DIF-donated receipt schemes, the Omega and AttestMCP papers) validate every concept here while lacking enforcement, trust-ladder productization, verification UX, and go-to-market. The durable moat is structural: matching "run verify yourself" requires incumbents to open-source their gateway core and re-architect key custody — strategically expensive for closed platforms — while the open receipt format, deliberately donated to a standards body in Phase 3, makes OpenSteps the neutral reference implementation rather than one more proprietary log.

## 9. Roadmap with gates

Phase 0, weeks 1–4: build the MVP above and run the ten interviews in parallel; gate on the decision criteria in Section 7. Phase 1, months 2–6: transparency-log anchoring (Rung 2), Slack/Teams approval flows, hosted offering for design partners; target three design partners in the accounting domain, at least one being an agent vendor, and one external auditor who has personally run `verify` on a real chain. Phase 2, months 6–12: paid conversion ($10–20K MRR target), SOC 2 Type I for the hosted tier, SIEM export; build Rung 3 (Nitro Enclaves, reproducible builds, KMS attestation binding) if and only if its gate has fired. Phase 3, months 12–24: cross-org receipt exchange, AttestMCP-style mutual attestation, donation of the receipt format to DIF or the MCP spec process, and the beginning of the receipt-consumer product (auditor workbench, insurer API) — the line of business where long-term pricing power lives.

## 10. Business model, condensed

Open core, with the SDK, gateway, schema, and verifier permanently open source — non-negotiable, because the security claim depends on auditable code and openness is the distribution strategy. Revenue: hosted gateway priced per agent or receipt volume (roughly $0 / $500 / $2,500+ monthly tiers); enterprise self-hosted with attestation key management and support ($30–100K annually); and later, verification products sold to receipt consumers. Phase 0–1 runs on under $10K with cloud credits; a serious Phase 2 push implies a $500K–1M pre-seed, raised only after the gates fire.

## 11. Top risks

In order: demand risk — buyers settle for gateway logs plus SOC 2, making verifiability a feature, not a company (mitigated by the pre-committed interview gates and the incident-story bar); incumbent absorption (mitigated by the open standard, neutrality positioning, and partnership-first GTM); verification UX failure — if auditors can't run `verify` easily the differentiator dies (mitigated by treating the one-command verifier and a web verify page as first-class product, usability-tested on non-cryptographers); protocol drift, as MCP/A2A absorb attestation natively (mitigated by participating in the spec process and aiming to be the reference implementation); and founder-stage risk against funded teams (mitigated by the niche's 12–18 month window of being too small for horizontal players to chase, and by the open-source wedge converting low credibility into visible artifact).

## 12. Success outlook, final

As a generic agent-security gateway: under 5% — that race is run. As the verifiability tier defined here, with the vendor-first accounting beachhead and pre-committed gates: roughly 40–50% odds the open-source project achieves meaningful adoption and becomes a career-defining asset regardless of commercial outcome; 15–25% odds of a sustainable niche business at $1M+ ARR; single digits (~3–7%) for a venture-scale outcome, contingent on the receipt format becoming something insurers, auditors, or regulators ask for by name. The downside is unusually soft — an open, working receipt standard is precisely the portfolio that gets its author hired by the companies in Section 8 — and the cost of resolving the central uncertainty is now four weeks.
